Project 01 · Python · EDA · Segmentation
Customer Behavior Analysis
for ConnectaTel
End-to-end behavioral analysis of mobile service usage for ConnectaTel, a telecom operating in Mexico and Colombia. Three datasets merged and cleaned to build unified user profiles, detect outliers, and segment ~4,200 customers by age group and usage level — generating actionable commercial recommendations.
Python · Pandas · NumPy Seaborn · Matplotlib IQR Outlier Detection Customer Segmentation

Context

ConnectaTel needed to understand how customers actually use their mobile plans (calls and messages) to optimize pricing, detect anomalies, and target the right segments. The analysis integrates three source tables: plans.csv (plan catalog), users_latam.csv (4,200+ customers), and usage.csv (granular event-level call/message logs).

The main challenges were data quality — invalid age values (−999), 11% missing cities, 55% missing call durations — and building a clean unified user profile from raw event logs through aggregation and merging.

Key Findings

  • Adults (30–59) are the core segment at 50% of total users. This is ConnectaTel's primary revenue base and highest loyalty opportunity.
  • Young users under 30 represent less than 20% (~750 users) — critical gap given that this is the highest mobile-consuming demographic.
  • ~3,000 users classified as "Medium Use" (5–9 calls and messages). Under 500 users reach "High Use" — suggesting most plans are underutilized.
  • Basic plan dominates at 68% vs Premium at 31% — raises the strategic question of whether Premium is priced too high or differentiated too little.
  • Call duration has significant outliers detected via IQR method — extreme values warrant fraud review or data entry audit.
  • churn_date nulls (88.35%) are valid — confirmed as active users, not data errors. Treated as MAR-informed retention signal.

# ── IQR OUTLIER DETECTION ─────────────────────────
columnas_limites = ['cant_mensajes', 'cant_llamadas', 'cant_minutos_llamada']
for col in columnas_limites:
    Q1    = user_profile[col].quantile(0.25)
    Q3    = user_profile[col].quantile(0.75)
    IQR   = Q3 - Q1
    lower = Q1 - 1.5 * IQR
    upper = Q3 + 1.5 * IQR
    outliers = ((user_profile[col] > upper) | (user_profile[col] < lower)).sum()
    print(f"{col}: lower={lower:.1f}, upper={upper:.1f}, outliers={outliers}")

# ── USAGE SEGMENTATION ────────────────────────────
def classify_uso(row):
    l, m = row['cant_llamadas'], row['cant_mensajes']
    if pd.isna(l) or pd.isna(m): return 'Error en Datos'

Project 02 · SQL Analysis
Funnel & Retention Analysis
for MercadoLibre
End-to-end behavioral analysis of MercadoLibre users using real event-level data (Jan–Aug 2025). Built a 6-stage conversion funnel and cohort retention matrix segmented by country to identify critical drop-off points and loyalty patterns across Latin America.
SQL · CTEs · LEFT JOINs Funnel Analysis Cohort Retention TripleTen 2026

Context

MercadoLibre is Latin America's leading e-commerce platform. This project analyzed real user event logs from Jan 1 to Aug 31, 2025, covering the full purchase journey across 10 countries. Two databases were used: mercadolibre_funnel (conversion events) and mercadolibre_retention (user activity by day after signup).

The goal was to answer two business questions: Where does the funnel leak the most? and How well does the platform retain users over 28 days? All queries used CTEs with LEFT JOINs, segmented by country and cohort month.

Key Findings

  • Biggest drop-off: select_item → add_to_cart at −85.7% (76.9% → 11.0%). This single step loses nearly 9 in 10 users who showed product intent — the #1 revenue bottleneck.
  • Paraguay is the highest-risk country: 100% of users abandon at begin_checkout — zero reach payment or purchase. Likely a payment method or UX barrier.
  • Uruguay leads conversion with 4.5% purchase rate vs 1.25% global average — 3.6× better than the platform mean.
  • Mexico leads 28-day retention at 3.1% (D28), with best D14 and D21 rates across all countries (55.8% and 25.5% respectively). Only Peru edges it out at D28 with 3.2%.
  • The March 2025 cohort was the strongest overall: 87.7% D7, 56.8% D14, 26.6% D21 — the best 3-metric cohort in the dataset.
funnel_general.sql SQL 
-- Funnel general: tasa de conversión por etapa respecto al first_visit
WITH first_visit AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id
  FROM mercadolibre_funnel
  WHERE event_name = 'first_visit'
    AND event_date BETWEEN '2025-01-01' AND '2025-08-31'
),
select_item AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id
  FROM mercadolibre_funnel
  WHERE event_name IN ('select_item', 'select_promotion')
    AND event_date BETWEEN '2025-01-01' AND '2025-08-31'
),
add_to_cart AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id FROM mercadolibre_funnel
  WHERE event_name = 'add_to_cart'
    AND event_date BETWEEN '2025-01-01' AND '2025-08-31'
),
begin_checkout AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id FROM mercadolibre_funnel
  WHERE event_name = 'begin_checkout'
    AND event_date BETWEEN '2025-01-01' AND '2025-08-31'
),
add_payment_info AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id FROM mercadolibre_funnel
  WHERE event_name = 'add_payment_info'
    AND event_date BETWEEN '2025-01-01' AND '2025-08-31'
),
purchase AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id FROM mercadolibre_funnel
  WHERE event_name = 'purchase'
    AND event_date BETWEEN '2025-01-01' AND '2025-08-31'
),
funnel_counts AS (
  SELECT
    COUNT(fv.user_id)  AS usuarios_first_visit,
    COUNT(si.user_id)  AS usuarios_select_item,
    COUNT(a.user_id)   AS usuarios_add_to_cart,
    COUNT(bc.user_id)  AS usuarios_begin_checkout,
    COUNT(api.user_id) AS usuarios_add_payment_info,
    COUNT(p.user_id)   AS usuarios_purchase
  FROM first_visit fv
  LEFT JOIN select_item si   ON fv.user_id = si.user_id
  LEFT JOIN add_to_cart a    ON fv.user_id = a.user_id
  LEFT JOIN begin_checkout bc ON fv.user_id = bc.user_id
  LEFT JOIN add_payment_info api ON fv.user_id = api.user_id
  LEFT JOIN purchase p       ON fv.user_id = p.user_id
)
SELECT
  ROUND(usuarios_select_item  * 100.0 / NULLIF(usuarios_first_visit, 0), 2) AS conversion_select_item,
  ROUND(usuarios_add_to_cart  * 100.0 / NULLIF(usuarios_first_visit, 0), 2) AS conversion_add_to_cart,
  ROUND(usuarios_begin_checkout * 100.0 / NULLIF(usuarios_first_visit, 0), 2) AS conversion_begin_checkout,
  ROUND(usuarios_add_payment_info * 100.0 / NULLIF(usuarios_first_visit, 0), 2) AS conversion_add_payment_info,
  ROUND(usuarios_purchase     * 100.0 / NULLIF(usuarios_first_visit, 0), 2) AS conversion_purchase
FROM funnel_counts;

-- Result: 76.90 | 11.01 | 4.00 | 2.09 | 1.25
cohort_retention.sql SQL 
-- Retención por cohorte: usuarios activos en D7, D14, D21, D28
WITH cohort AS (
  SELECT
    user_id,
    TO_CHAR(DATE_TRUNC('month', MIN(signup_date)), 'YYYY-MM') AS cohort
  FROM mercadolibre_retention
  GROUP BY user_id
),
activity AS (
  SELECT
    r.user_id,
    c.cohort,
    r.day_after_signup,
    r.active
  FROM mercadolibre_retention r
  LEFT JOIN cohort c ON r.user_id = c.user_id
  WHERE r.activity_date BETWEEN '2025-01-01' AND '2025-08-31'
)
SELECT
  cohort,
  ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN day_after_signup >= 7  AND active = 1 THEN user_id END) / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT user_id), 0), 1) AS retention_d7_pct,
  ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN day_after_signup >= 14 AND active = 1 THEN user_id END) / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT user_id), 0), 1) AS retention_d14_pct,
  ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN day_after_signup >= 21 AND active = 1 THEN user_id END) / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT user_id), 0), 1) AS retention_d21_pct,
  ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN day_after_signup >= 28 AND active = 1 THEN user_id END) / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT user_id), 0), 1) AS retention_d28_pct
FROM activity
GROUP BY cohort
ORDER BY cohort;

-- Result sample: 2025-03 → D7: 87.7% | D14: 56.8% | D21: 26.6% | D28: 3.0%

WITH first_visits AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id, country
  FROM mercadolibre_funnel
  WHERE event_name = 'first_visit'
    AND event_date BETWEEN '2025-01-01' AND '2025-08-31'
),
-- [CTEs restantes: select_item, add_to_cart, begin_checkout,
--  add_shipping_info, add_payment_info, purchase — mismo patrón + country]
funnel_counts AS (
  SELECT
    fv.country,
    COUNT(fv.user_id)  AS usuarios_first_visit,
    COUNT(si.user_id)  AS usuarios_select_item,
    COUNT(a.user_id)   AS usuarios_add_to_cart,
    COUNT(bc.user_id)  AS usuarios_begin_checkout,

  LEFT JOIN add_to_cart a   ON fv.user_id = a.user_id   AND fv.country = a.country
  LEFT JOIN begin_checkout bc ON fv.user_id = bc.user_id AND fv.country = bc.country
  LEFT JOIN purchase p      ON fv.user_id = p.user_id   AND fv.country = p.country
  GROUP BY fv.country
)
SELECT
  country,
  ROUND(usuarios_select_item   * 100.0 / NULLIF(usuarios_first_visit, 0), 2) AS conv_select_item,

  ROUND(usuarios_purchase      * 100.0 / NULLIF(usuarios_first_visit, 0), 2) AS conv_purchase
FROM funnel_counts
ORDER BY conv_purchase DESC;

Project 03 · Python · EDA
Urban Mobility & Economic
Productivity in LATAM
Exploratory data analysis of 15 major Latin American cities combining TomTom Traffic Index (real-time congestion) with OECD Cities data (GDP per capita, unemployment, PM2.5). Goal: identify where to invest in transport infrastructure to maximize economic and social impact.
Python · Pandas · NumPy Matplotlib · Seaborn EDA · Data Merging TripleTen 2026

Context

Latin American cities face a paradox: rapid urbanization drives economic growth while simultaneously creating severe traffic congestion that erodes productivity. This project analyzed 2024 data from two real sources — TomTom Traffic Index and OECD Cities — merged and cleaned with Pandas to produce a unified 15-city dataset.

The dataset includes congestion metrics (traffic index, jams delay, jams count, km blocked), economic indicators (GDP per capita, unemployment %), air quality (PM2.5), and population. A correlation matrix was computed across all numerical variables.

Key Findings

  • Bogotá has the highest traffic index (37.6) — worst congestion in the dataset — yet ranks only 10th in GDP. Classic congestion trap: high friction, moderate output.
  • Mexico City is the paradox: 2nd highest traffic index (34.2) but 2nd best GDP ($21,111). High economic activity sustains itself despite — or because of — density.
  • Montevideo is the efficiency benchmark: lowest congestion (17.0), highest GDP ($26,176), low PM2.5 (11.2). Best combined score in the dataset.
  • Correlation between traffic index and GDP is near zero (r = 0.056) — no linear relationship. Cities develop through multiple pathways, not just mobility.
  • Santiago is the outlier: high congestion (20.2) but lowest GDP ($2,277) — the worst efficiency ratio in the dataset. Structural investment needed.
data_loading_cleaning.py Python 
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Load two source datasets
traffic = pd.read_csv('tomtom_traffic.csv')
eco     = pd.read_csv('oecd_city_economy.csv')

# Standardize column names
traffic.rename(columns={
    'City':'city', 'Country':'country',
    'JamsDelay':'jams_delay', 'TrafficIndexLive':'traffic_index_live',
    'JamsLengthInKms':'jams_length_kms', 'JamsCount':'jams_count',
    'MinsDelay':'mins_delay',
    'TravelTimeLivePer10KmsMins':'travel_time_live_per_10kms_mins',
    'TravelTimeHistoricPer10KmsMins':'travel_time_hist_per_10kms_mins'
}, inplace=True)

eco.rename(columns={
    'City GDP/capita':'city_gdp_capita', 'Unemployment %':'unemployment_pct',
    'Country':'country', 'Year':'year', 'City':'city',
    'Population (M)':'population_m', 'PM2.5 (μg/m³)':'pm25'
}, inplace=True)

# Convert numeric fields (handle comma separators)
for col in ['city_gdp_capita', 'unemployment_pct', 'population_m']:
    eco[col] = (eco[col].astype('string')
                         .str.strip()
                         .str.replace('.', '', regex=False)
                         .str.replace(',', '.', regex=False))
    eco[col] = pd.to_numeric(eco[col], errors='coerce')

eco['population'] = eco['population_m'] * 1_000_000

# Convert timestamps, extract year, filter 2024
traffic['update_time_utc'] = pd.to_datetime(traffic['update_time_utc'], errors='coerce', utc=True)
traffic['year'] = traffic['update_time_utc'].dt.year

traffic_2024 = traffic[traffic['year'] == 2024].copy()
eco_2024     = eco[eco['year']     == 2024].copy()

# Aggregate traffic by city-year (daily snapshots → annual averages)
traffic_agg = (
    traffic_2024
    .groupby(['country', 'city', 'year'])
    .agg({
        'jams_delay':                      'mean',
        'traffic_index_live':             'mean',

        'travel_time_live_per_10kms_mins':'mean',

    })
    .reset_index()

# Merge traffic + economy on city + year
merged = pd.merge(
    traffic_agg[['city','country','year','jams_delay',

                  'travel_time_hist_per_10kms_mins']],
    eco_2024[['city','year','city_gdp_capita',

)

# Correlation matrix across all numeric variables
corr_matrix = merged.select_dtypes(include=['float64', 'int64']).corr()
print(corr_matrix['traffic_index_live'])

Main Conclusions

  • No direct correlation between traffic and GDP (r = 0.056). Urban productivity is multi-causal. Congestion alone does not explain economic performance — institutional quality, industry mix, and infrastructure investment all play roles.
  • Mexico City defies the congestion trap: highest jams_delay (2,833) and 2nd highest GDP ($21,111). The city's economic density sustains output despite friction. However, investment in mobility would unlock further productivity gains.
  • Santiago is the most inefficient city in the dataset: congestion index 20.2 (above median) but the lowest GDP ($2,277). This suggests structural economic issues beyond mobility — yet transport investment would reduce friction costs directly.
  • Montevideo is the template: lowest traffic index (17.0), highest GDP ($26,176), best PM2.5 (11.2 μg/m³). Compact urban design + strong public transit = efficiency. Replicable in cities of similar scale.
  • PM2.5 correlates strongly with traffic (r = 0.631) — congestion is not just an economic issue but a public health emergency. São Paulo (28.0 μg/m³) and Bogotá (17.6) have the highest pollution in the dataset.

Investment priority matrix: São Paulo and Bogotá require immediate infrastructure action (high congestion + health risk). Santiago needs structural economic + mobility co-investment. Mexico City should focus on demand management (congestion pricing, modal shift) rather than new road capacity. Montevideo and Brasilia serve as benchmarks for mid-size city planning.
